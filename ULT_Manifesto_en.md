## 1.Unity
### 1.1.Basic Background
#### 1.1.1.Profit-seeking and Harm-avoiding Instinct
Terrans(human beings) are a kind of animal. They began to stand out from other organisms about 50 million years ago and gradually developed into the ruler of the earth.  
50 million years is not too long in the long long evolution of organisms.  
Therefore, humans share quite a few Commonalities with the vast majority of animals that have not yet been eliminated by natural selection.  
The most prominent of these is the profit-seeking and harm-avoiding instinct; animals lacking this instinct have almost all become extinct.

At birth, human nature is inherently evil.  
Many people believe that `at birth, human nature is inherently good` and that humans possess a `humanity` that other animals lack, drawing a clear distinction between themselves and the savagery of other animals.  
However, due to animal instincts, humans are naturally endowed with an `evil` nature since they were born.  
This `evil` is not purely a derogatory term, but rather `for profit-seeking and harm-avoiding, ignoring the interests of others`.

But why do most people behave in a way that reflects their `humanity` and clearly distinguishes them from beasts?  
I do not deny the existence of humanity; I just believe it is not an instinct but something acquired through growing up in human society and learning by example.  
Very strong evidences are [feral child](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feral_child) and [Chantek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chantek).  
Although feral children are human, they were raised by other animals from a young age and have adopted the habits of those animals entirely, making it hard to summarize the word `humanity` from them.  
Although the orangutan Shatke is not human, Shatke was raised as a human, so it is easy to observe the word `humanity` in him. However, because it was less intelligent than others, it ultimately never learned to speak, and eventually had its human rights stripped away due to violent behavior that revealed its inhuman savagery. The outcome was distressing, but for most of that long period, it displayed humanity; it showed its savagery only once.There are multiple reasons for this negative outcome. First, because it is not human, people hold it to higher standards, and it is inevitable that it will face discrimination and feel out of place in human society. Consequently, it can successfully integrate countless times, but as long as it makes a mistake once, almost everyone will speculate on it with the worst imagination. Second, the female classmate’s allegations of violence against it are unsubstantiated. I am not saying that Shateke must have been framed, but any human who wants can make baseless accusations against it, and such accusations are easily accepted, because people are naturally more inclined to believe their own kind than to believe Shatke. Third, it is not human after all, and it is unlikely to give it a fully human identity in human society. It will inevitably fall into the psychological struggle of identity and it is difficult not to be depressed.

Since `at birth, human nature is inherently good`, why is most of human society now showing the brilliance of humanity?  
I believe that tens of millions of years ago, human society must have been little different from animal herds, and the same kind of people hunted each other savagely for various interests.  
But the wisdom derived from the human brain is ultimately different from beasts'. After a long period of bloodshed, some ancient sages—who had gained greater mastery over their own wisdom—realized the destructive power of mutual slaughter and that cooperation brought greater benefits than plunder. Thus, they began to extend cooperation that existed only between spouses and blood relatives to a larger human race, from which the rudiments of `humanity` emerged, and then this extra-instinctive characteristic is passed down from generation to generation by memes rather than genes through word of mouth.  
Gradually expanding from the family to the wider community, family -> tribe -> country.

`humanity` can make people reluctant to infringe on the interests of others to a certain extent.  
However, `reluctant to infringe on the interests of others` do have a range decay, the closer one is to oneself, the less willing one is to infringe upon his interests. From close to far: spouse and blood relatives -> neighbourhood -> same hometown and customs -> similar dialects -> same ethnic group -> similar appearance characteristics -> same country -> same language -> foreigner  
Therefore, the most perfect humanity has not spread to the whole human society. Many different countries, forces, parties, groups and people do not hesitate to infringe on the interests of others to obtain their own interests.  
It is difficult for acquired habits to completely suppress innate instincts.
#### 1.1.2.The Contingency of History
Many political science theories with widespread influence, such as the [End of History](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_of_history) and Marxism, hold that the trend of history is inevitable; the debate is just what the end of history should be.  
But I think history is inherently driven by contingency.  
Such as the [Soviet nuclear submarine in the Cuban missile crisis](#a1soviet-nuclear-submarine-in-the-cuban-missile-crisis) incident, a lot of historical trends have been driven by the chance occurrence of rare events such as a few people overturning the majority.  
A more convincing example is that the emergence of human beings from animals is itself a small probability event of evolution, and it is obviously unrealistic to expect animal society to go to the inevitable end of those theories; the fact that Earth became a place capable of sustaining life is an even small probability event.

Of course, I do not deny that in specific historical periods, there may be many high probability regularities, but most of those regularities are effective only during stable development stages. When the development of these regularities approaches its limits, society becomes unstable, giving rise to a multitude of low probability paths—each of which can drive history toward a radically different course. **It is precisely these low probability events that drive history to leap into different periods, thereby manifesting different high probability regularities**. Transitional periods of change are often shorter than periods of stable historical development, which is why it is all too easy to fall into the illusion of inevitable trend.
